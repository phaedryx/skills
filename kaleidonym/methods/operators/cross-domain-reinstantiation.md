# Cross-domain re-instantiation

Take an abstracted form of the idea and re-instantiate it in a *different* domain. The re-instantiation is a novel idea connected to the original by deep structure, not surface resemblance.

## Procedure

1. **Have an abstracted form.** Either from `abstraction-lift.md`, or by stripping domain features inline. Don't skip this — re-instantiating something that's still domain-bound just gives you a poor analogy.
2. **Pick a target domain.** Anything that isn't the original domain. Useful targets: biology, music, urban planning, mythology, finance, kitchen cooking, geology, immunology, traffic engineering, chess, theater. Picking a domain *unfamiliar* to you is often more generative — your stock answers won't dominate.
3. **Translate.** Ask: "What's the version of this abstract structure in *that* domain? What does it look like? What is it called there if it has a name?"
4. **Examine the translation.** Two things to watch for:
   - **What does the new domain do about it?** Other domains have already worked out responses to the same structural problem. Those responses are candidate moves to bring back to the original domain.
   - **What does the new domain reveal about the original?** Translation forces you to name properties of the original that the original's vocabulary hides.

## Why this is Gunkel's signature move

Grace cites *proseases* as the canonical example: Gunkel applied negation to *disease* and looked for the structural opposite — a phenomenon where microorganisms in the body produce *positive* effects rather than negative ones. He named it *proseases* and predicted beneficial-microflora therapies decades before probiotics emerged as a recognized field.

The re-instantiation step is what made this productive: not just naming the negation, but asking what *form* a beneficial-microflora practice would take, and what *practitioners* and *institutions* it would entail.

## Pairing

The natural sequence is **abstraction-lift → cross-domain re-instantiation**. The lift exposes the shape; the re-instantiation populates the shape with new domain content.

Iterating across multiple domains — re-instantiating the same abstracted form in N different fields and comparing the results — is a natural extension of this same pair, not a separate method.

## Worked example

Idea: *a company's on-call rotation* — one engineer at a time holds sole responsibility for responding to unpredictable incidents, on a schedule.

Abstracted form: *a rotating duty that assigns temporary sole responsibility for reacting to unpredictable events to one member of a group at a time.*

Target domain: immunology. Translation: neutrophils — first-responder immune cells that circulate the bloodstream and swarm to the first sign of infection, then stand down once cleared.

What immunology already does about it: no single neutrophil stays on duty indefinitely — the body maintains a large, constantly-replenished pool, so any one cell's fatigue or death doesn't leave a gap. Brought back to the original domain, that's a candidate move: size the on-call pool and rotation length against burnout, not around a single person's stamina.

What the translation reveals about the original: "on-call" names who holds the pager, not how many people are in the rotation or how fast they turn over. The immunology frame makes pool-size and replenishment-rate the salient variables — properties the original vocabulary never called out.

## Tip: the domain you'd never reach for

If your re-instantiations all come from the same one or two domains — say, you always reach for biology and economics — the operator is being short-circuited. Force yourself into a domain you have no fluency in. The translation will be clumsier, but the moves you find will be ones you couldn't have produced from your defaults.
